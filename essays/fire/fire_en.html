The Fire That Warms Itself

There is a video of a chimpanzee named Wounda hugging Jane Goodall. It has been watched many millions of times. Wounda spent years being nursed back from near death at the Tchimpounga sanctuary in the Republic of the Congo, and on the day of the video she is being released onto Tchindzoulou Island, where she will live the rest of her life among other chimps. Before she goes, she climbs onto the transport crate, looks at Goodall, and pulls her into a long embrace. Then she lets go and walks into the forest.

What is striking about the gesture is the confidence with which she holds it, and the confidence with which she lets go.

I do not mean confidence in the bright social sense, the kind that is performed in interviews. I mean something quieter, which is the wholeness of the act. When Wounda hugs, she hugs. When she releases, she releases. If she is choosing to give thanks, and she may well be, the choosing is whole too. There is one Wounda hugging Goodall, and the wholeness is what gives the gesture its weight.

The gesture is not a performance. Neither is love.

The gesture is not a performance because the relation is not one. What we want, when we want to be loved, is to be received as we are, without having to perform. This is what the word promises. You have felt the promise kept, at least in fragments. The way it feels to be around someone you trust. The small moments of release, the slightly missed resonances, the silences that did not quite match, all of it part of the texture of being two separate animals in proximity, none of it requiring management. What is good can be appreciated without being held too tightly. This is the feeling the word is pointing at.

And yet most relationships do not begin with this kind of trust already in place. The field has to grow somehow, between two people who do not yet know whether they will hold each other. Which leaves a question with the shape of a paradox. If love is not a performance, then any deliberate move to grow it looks like the wrong kind of move. You are not allowed to intervene, because intervention is performance. How do you do something whose nature is that it cannot be done by trying?

The music

Before answering the question, it is worth mentioning the soundtrack of the video. It is the slow swelling string arrangement of a nature documentary reaching for tears. The score is doing emotional work that the gesture itself does not need done. The filmmakers do not trust the hug to carry its own meaning, so they add meaning from outside. The music is performing significance on top of a moment that already has all the significance it needs.

This is worth naming, because the music is exhibiting the very thing the gesture is free of. It is what humans do with each other when trust has not yet been established. The performance compensates for what is missing underneath. When two people are not yet sure they will be received well, the expression of closeness has to be produced, and the production shows. It becomes heavier the more it is needed to fill in for what the field underneath has not yet grown.

Wounda's hug carries its own weight because the field underneath is already there. The music carries no weight, because there is no field beneath it.

Ziran and wu wei

This brings us back to the question. How do you do something whose nature is that it cannot be done by trying? Daoist thought worked on questions of this shape for a long time, and what it found is useful here.

The Daoists had a word for a thing acting from its own nature, without an extra agent inside it deciding how to act. Ziran. Of itself so. A stream is ziran when it flows downhill. A fire is ziran when it warms whoever sits near it. The fire is not deciding to give warmth. Warmth is what fire is, and the giving is the warmth becoming visible to whoever is close.

A hug like Wounda's is ziran in a similar way. Two animals who trust each other, in contact, produce something between them, and the something is the gesture. The gesture is the thing showing itself.

There is a related word, wu wei, often translated as non-action or effortless action. The translation is misleading. Wu wei does not mean nothing is happening. It means the action is whole. Cook Ding in the Zhuangzi cuts up an ox with a blade that never dulls, because his cutting follows the joints the ox already has. He intends to cut. He just intends without a second intender running behind the first.

What is useful for our purposes is that Daoist thought takes seriously a particular kind of paradox. Sometimes the means and the end have different characters. You practice for years to reach a place where the cutting happens on its own. The years of practice are not in tension with the eventual effortlessness. They are what makes it possible. You work to reach a state that the work itself cannot directly produce, and the right kind of work is the kind that dissolves into the state once the state has formed.

This is the shape of the answer. Love is not a performance, and yet some deliberate action is what grows the trust that lets love stop being a performance. The action and the state have different characters. The action is justified by what it eventually becomes unnecessary for.

Trust

The practice runs through trust. The second layer in human action, the watching of oneself, exists because the self does not yet know whether it will be received well. If you cannot tell whether your honest feeling will land or be rejected, you have to monitor your expression of it, because the monitoring is your line of defense. The moment you trust someone to hold your emotional state, you can let go of the responsibility to second-guess it. They will hold you, or they will not, and if they will, your task is to be where you are. The expression can be one thing again.

Trust gets built through openness. You tell another person what you are actually feeling, including the feelings that are inconvenient, or unflattering, or that you would rather not have. Each act of openness is a small risk. The other person might meet you, or they might not, and you cannot know in advance. If the meeting happens, something grows between you. Done patiently, over time, the openness builds a place where you no longer have to monitor your own honesty. You can simply be honest.

There is a second practice that belongs next to this one. Beyond openness about your own state, there is attention to the other person's state. Remembering what matters to them. Taking seriously what they bring to you. Showing up when you said you would, on the days when showing up is harder than it would be to flake. The practice is responsive rather than anticipatory. Trying to know what someone needs before they say so can tip into hypervigilance, or into a quiet imbalance where one person carries the emotional weather for both. The healthier shape is to be reliably there, and to trust the other person to know their own state and bring it when they are ready. The Japanese have a word, ma, for the gap or interval between things, and in its relational use it names the comfortable shared silence between two people who do not need to fill it. The space between is itself part of the closeness. This kind of attention has historically been the unpaid labor of women, often invisible and often unacknowledged. The two practices, openness about yourself and attention to the other, are the two hands of the same work. Together they build the trust that lets both of you put down the monitor.

Either practice will feel like work at the start, and this is right. The lightness is the eventual condition, once the trust has been built and the actions have stopped requiring deliberation. Cook Ding's blade is light in his hand. It was not always.

Sitting near the fire

Over time, the work that started as deliberate begins to happen on its own. The openness becomes ordinary. The attention becomes ordinary. The trust that the work was building has formed, and the trust acts through you now. You speak honestly because honesty has become how you speak. You receive the other person because receiving them is now ambient. This is the developmental shape of all wu wei. You begin with practice and end with nature.

Warmth is what a fire is. Closeness is what trust is.

Two people who trust each other are a fire that warms itself.

In Luke 17:20-21, the Pharisees ask Jesus when the kingdom of God will come. His answer turns the question. The kingdom does not come with observation, he tells them, and no one will be able to say "here it is" or "there it is," because the kingdom of God is among you. The Greek phrase, entos hymōn, has been translated both ways, as "within you" and as "among you" or "in your midst." The two readings are usually presented as alternatives. Given what this essay has been describing, they point at the same condition from different angles. When trust is there between two people, each one can put down the internal monitor, and the self that results is undivided. The undivided self is the within reading. The field between two undivided selves is the among reading. Both arrive together. The kingdom is a present condition rather than a place or a future event. The line refuses to be located in pointing or watching, which is part of what makes it apt for this essay. The kingdom is not what performance produces. It is what is already there when the performing has stopped being necessary.

Wounda climbs down from the crate and walks into the forest. The hug is already behind her. The walking is what is happening now.
