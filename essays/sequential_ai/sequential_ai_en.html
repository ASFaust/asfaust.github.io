In Defense of Sequential AI Models

Sequential models lost. Everyone knows this. The recurrent network, which read its input one step at a time and carried a state forward, was overtaken by the transformer, which reads everything at once and lets every position consult every other. I want to defend sequential models anyway. But only one thing about them, and not the thing you are expecting me to defend.

The word "sequential" hides two ideas that have been welded together, and almost every argument against sequential models blames the wrong one. The first idea is the forward dependency: the state at step t is computed from the state at step t-1. The second is the backward unroll: to figure out how the past should have been different, you retrace the entire forward trajectory in reverse. When people say sequential models do not scale, they are talking about the second. When they abandon the recurrent state, they throw out the first along with it. This essay defends the first and hands you the second to discard. The argument is that the forward dependency is the part worth keeping, that it is not a property of recurrent networks at all but of causal computation as such, and that the backward unroll can be replaced rather than merely shrunk, with biology standing as proof that the replacement is possible.

I. What "sequential" means

Start with the forward dependency, because it is the part people are most willing to give up and least able to actually escape.

To compute a function of a process that unfolds in time is to compute each output from the state that preceded it. State t depends on state t-1, which depended on t-2, and so on back to the beginning. This is what a computation over a temporal process is. Causality runs one way, and any system that respects it inherits a one-way dependency in its computation. You can call the carried-forward quantity a hidden state, a residual stream, or a cache, and the dependency remains.

The transformer looks like a counterexample, and it is worth seeing why it is not. An autoregressive transformer masks attention so that position t attends only to positions up to t. The output at t is therefore a function of the past and the present, and the arrow points forward exactly as it does in a recurrent network. The transformer parallelizes the computation of these forward-dependent outputs across positions, which is a real and valuable trick, but the dependency structure it computes is forward all the same. The arrow survives the parallelization. This matters because the forward dependency is what I am defending, and the most successful architecture in the field already obeys it.

Now the backward unroll. To train any of these models by gradient descent, you need to know how a change to an early state would have changed a late output. Backpropagation through time answers this by walking the trajectory in reverse, multiplying Jacobians step by step from the output back to the input. It is exact, it is general, and it is the most expensive thing in the room. It demands that you store the entire forward trajectory so you can revisit it, which makes memory grow with sequence length. It demands that the reverse walk happen in order, since each step's gradient depends on the next step's, which makes the backward pass as sequential as the forward one and unable to begin until the forward pass has finished.

Here is the distinction that organizes everything that follows. The forward dependency is constitutive. It is a fact about what computing over time is, and causality forbids us from removing it. The backward unroll is a method. It is one way to assign credit, chosen for its generality, and generality is precisely why it is expensive. The first is ontological. The second is a decision. The field has spent a decade optimizing the second as though it were the first, and the title of this essay is a plea to separate them: keep the arrow, drop the unroll.

II. The transformer as the road not taken

The transformer pushed hardest against the forward constraint without escaping it, and the marks of that effort are visible in its design. Read them as the cost of a strategy. Three are worth naming. First, computational width is welded to input length: a standard transformer carries one position per input token through every layer, fusing how much there is to compute about with how much room the computation needs, so a trivial and a hard problem over the same input get identical working memory. The Perceiver and the Mamba family break the weld by giving the computation a fixed width of its own, which shows the coupling was a contingency of hardware batching and caching rather than a necessity. Second, a position is not its token. Logit-lens work finds early layers projecting to the current token and late layers to the next-token prediction, while the middle layers decode to neither, because the middle of the network parks computation at a position indexed by when it sits rather than which token it names. Attention sinks are the sharp case: models dump attention onto early positions as scratch registers whose token identity is irrelevant. Third, the causal mask freezes the past. Once position t is computed, a later token cannot revise it, so a correction must be represented in later positions and carried forward. Read "the old man the boats" and a human reparses; the transformer carries the corrected parse forward instead. The architecture built to let every position see every other still propagates corrections in the one direction causality allows.

There is a fourth mark, and it names the others' shared currency. The only axis along which a transformer accumulates genuinely serial computation is depth. Within a layer everything is parallel, so one layer buys one step of composition, a step that may depend on the result of the previous step. A nested function f(a, f(b, f(c, f(d, …)))) of composition depth k therefore needs at least k stacked layers to be computed faithfully, since each f must consume the result of the one inside it. With fewer layers there is no route for the serial dependency, and the model can approximate or memorize but not compute. This is the content of the bounded-depth expressivity results: a fixed-depth transformer lives in a shallow circuit class, and functions whose serial depth exceeds the layer count fall outside it.

That fixes the deeper picture. Serial composition depth is a single quantity that both architectures must pay, on different axes. An RNN accumulates it along time, one step per token, so its budget runs effectively unbounded while its gradients travel long, ill-conditioned paths through that many sequential Jacobians. A transformer accumulates it along depth, one step per layer, so its budget is small and fixed while its gradient paths stay short and well conditioned, and its layers compute in parallel because each depends on the layer below rather than on its neighbors. The transformer relocated serial computation from the time axis to the depth axis, bought parallelism and conditioning with the move, and capped its depth at the layer count as the price. Chain-of-thought is how it pays past the cap: emitting an intermediate result as a token writes a partial computation back into the input, so the next forward pass consumes it and the autoregressive loop supplies serial steps the layer stack cannot. This unifies the two things chain-of-thought does. Laying down legible step-by-step reasoning is the mechanism that recovers serial depth through the loop, and the bare width effect, where appending content-free filler tokens helps while prepending them does not, is the same recovery operating when the content is empty and only the fresh positions matter.

So the bill is clear, and it sets up the road not taken. The transformer kept serial computation shallow and wide, which makes credit assignment short and parallel, and capped its depth to do so. A forward-only program makes the opposite trade: fold the past into a carried-forward summary, which keeps the dependency graph deep in time and the gradient path long. It recovers neither the transformer's shallowness nor its parallelism for free, and assuming it does is the easiest way to be wrong about it. What it dodges is the other cost, the memory and the exactness of the backward unroll. The road is to carry the past forward and make that carry good enough to learn from. The rest of this essay is about that road.

III. What the backward unroll actually buys

It would be easy to treat backpropagation through time as pure waste, and that would be a mistake. The backward unroll buys something real, and any honest replacement has to account for what it is buying.

In a general recurrent system, the present state is an entangled function of the entire past. A perturbation to a state five hundred steps ago propagates through every intervening nonlinearity and arrives at the present smeared across many dimensions, its influence tangled with the influence of everything that happened in between. To recover how much that distant perturbation mattered, you have to account for the whole intervening chain, because the answer genuinely depends on it. This is what the reverse walk computes. It is not retracing the trajectory out of habit. It is retracing it because, in the general case, the information needed to assign credit for a long-range dependency is distributed across the trajectory and cannot be read off from the present state alone.

The consequence is a real obstacle, and I want to state it plainly rather than wave it away. A learning rule that looks only at quantities available now, at the current step, in the forward direction, cannot be exactly correct for arbitrary temporal dependencies. The exact gradient depends on the intervening chain, and a purely local rule does not have access to the chain. So any forward-only method is an approximation, and the question is not whether it is exact but whether it is accurate on the dependencies that matter. The cost of exactness is the memory that grows with the sequence and the reverse pass that cannot start until the forward pass ends. The bet of this essay is that exactness, bought at that price, is more than the problem requires.

IV. The existence proof and the change of basis

There is a system that assigns temporal credit well, learns from a stream it has already partly forgotten, updates as it goes, and stores no trajectory to walk backward through. It is the brain, and it is the reason to take the forward-only road seriously.

I want to be careful about what biology proves, because the careful version is stronger than the loose one. Biology does not prove that any particular forward-only learning rule works, and it does not hand over a mechanism. What it proves is an existence claim: effective temporal credit assignment is achievable with constant memory in the length of the stream, forward-only, online. The brain does not freeze and replay the last ten thousand moments in reverse. Whatever it does, it does while moving forward, carrying a bounded amount of state. So anyone who claims that the backward unroll is necessary, that there is no way to learn long-range temporal structure without retracing the trajectory, is refuted by a working example. I cannot prove that my construction succeeds. I can prove that the thing it attempts is possible, because something is already doing it. That is the most an existence proof can offer, and it is exactly what is needed: it shows the design space is non-empty.

What would such a mechanism look like? The obstacle in the previous section was entanglement: in a general system the past arrives at the present smeared and tangled, so credit cannot be read off locally. The way through is to change the coordinates in which the state is represented so that the entanglement comes apart. If the influence of the past on the present can be made approximately diagonal, so that each stored piece of the past affects the present along its own direction without crosstalk into the others, then the credit for that piece can be read off locally. The reverse walk existed to untangle the smear. Pick a basis where the smear is already untangled, and the walk has nothing left to do.

This is not a wish. It is a concrete and measurable property of how the state is encoded. When the directions along which the past is stored are close to orthogonal, the stored pieces do not interfere when the present reads from them, and the same near-orthogonality that keeps retrieval clean also keeps credit assignment clean, since both are asking how much one stored direction bleeds into another. Near-orthogonal storage directions make the temporal influence approximately diagonal, which is the condition under which a forward-local rule approximates the true gradient. High dimension helps for free, since random directions in a high-dimensional space are nearly orthogonal to begin with. The architecture's job, then, is to project the learning problem into coordinates where the stored directions decorrelate, and in those coordinates the cheap forward rule becomes accurate.

So the bet of the essay can be stated as something falsifiable. The temporal dependencies that matter for real sequence learning live in a subspace where a forward-carried summary is sufficient, because in the right basis their influence on the present is approximately local. When this holds, forward-only credit assignment is accurate and the backward unroll is redundant. When it fails, it will fail on dependencies that are irreducibly tangled and long-range, and characterizing exactly where that boundary sits is the scientific content of the program, more than any single architecture that lives on the good side of it.

V. Where the basis comes from

The previous section leaned on a basis in which the forward rule works, and owes an account of where such a basis comes from. The honest answer reaches past the lifetime of a single learner.

Finding coordinates that decorrelate the temporal structure of an entire class of problems is hard. It is the expensive part, and it is the kind of search that a single forward pass over a single stream cannot perform, because the right basis is a fact about the distribution of problems rather than about any one sequence. This is the natural place to put a slow outer optimization. Evolution plays exactly this role in biology: it searches, over a timescale far longer than any individual life, for representational priors that make within-life learning tractable. The basis is not rediscovered from scratch by each brain through expensive backward credit assignment. It is inherited, compiled into the starting point, so that the fast online rule that runs during a life is cheap precisely because the coordinates it operates in were already chosen to make it cheap.

This gives a clean division of labor across two timescales. The slow loop, evolutionary in spirit, searches for the basis and the priors and pays the high cost of that search once. The fast loop, the forward-local rule, does lifetime learning inside the inherited coordinates and stays cheap because the hard structural work is already done. Seen this way, backpropagation through time is the method that refuses the prior. It assumes nothing about the structure of the problem and so re-derives the entanglement from raw trajectory every single sequence, paying the full general-case cost over and over. Its generality, the very thing that makes it exact, is the thing that makes it wasteful, because it declines to inherit what could have been inherited.

There is an open question here, and it is the one the program turns on, so I will end on it rather than paper over it. Biology gets its basis from a billion years of selection against the actual structure of the world, and it works because the world is consistent enough across deep time for the inherited priors to keep paying off in situations the organism has never seen. An artificial outer loop has neither that span nor that richness. It searches under whatever distribution of tasks we hand it, in vastly less time, and the danger is that it finds a basis that fits those tasks and nothing beyond them. A real basis keeps the forward rule aligned with true credit assignment on dependencies it was never trained against. A brittle one only looks right in sample. Telling these apart, on held-out temporal structure, is the experiment the whole edifice rests on, and the quality of the task distribution we search under is what decides which one we get.

That open question is what gives the defense its edge. The forward dependency stays, because causality gives us an arrow and every computation over time runs along it. The backward unroll goes, because it is one method among possible methods, and biology proves the method has a replacement. What remains to be earned is the basis that makes the replacement accurate, and the conjecture, stated so it can be tested rather than believed, is that such a basis is the kind of thing a slow search can find and a fast learner can inherit. In defending sequential models, I am defending the arrow and asking us to stop paying, sequence after sequence, to walk it backward.
