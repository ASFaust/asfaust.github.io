On men, creation, and the architecture of domination

A philosophical and anthropological essay

There is an asymmetry at the root of human experience that rarely gets named directly. Women carry an undeniable, visceral relationship to the creation of life — written into the body, impossible to doubt. Men do not create life. They can only support it. This is not a moral judgment but a biological fact, and like most biological facts, it has consequences that reach far beyond the body into psychology, society, and history.

Every living being is shaped by evolutionary pressure toward proliferation — toward mattering, leaving a trace, continuing beyond oneself. But where a woman's participation in that process is self-evident, a man's is brief, invisible, and for most of human history impossible to verify with certainty. The result is an existential gap: a need to prove significance through other means. This gap is not a flaw or a pathology. It is a condition. What matters is what gets built in response to it.

In pre-agrarian societies, men found coherent answers to that gap. The mechanisms were not dominance hierarchies — in fact, hunter-gatherer bands actively suppressed would-be alphas through ridicule, ostracism, and leveling mechanisms that enforced a remarkable degree of equality. What made a man significant was skill and reliability: the hunter who consistently brought meat demonstrated his value in a way immediately legible to everyone in the band. Coalition mattered equally — a man embedded in a strong network of reciprocal relationships was more reproductively secure than any lone dominant male. Human sociality was always too complex for brute force to be the primary currency.

This is also, it turns out, what women still reliably find attractive — not dominance in the abstract, but its pre-agrarian precursors: a man who provides, and who is trusted and liked by those around him. These preferences were never merely cultural. They are deep evolutionary signal. A man who can provide and who is well-regarded by his community is a demonstrably good bet for offspring survival. The two criteria together are more reliable than physical strength alone, which is why they persisted across every transformation the human world has undergone since.

Even the marking of male identity within these societies reveals the asymmetry. A girl's transition into womanhood is announced by the body — undeniable, automatic, requiring nothing from the community. A boy's transition into manhood is not. It has to be manufactured. This is what initiation rites accomplish across so many cultures and epochs: the tribe steps in to do what biology omitted. Manhood is conferred through ordeal, witnessed, declared. It is a social act because it cannot be a biological one. Even here, male significance requires external recognition in a way female significance does not.

The question of paternity — which would later become so charged — was in many pre-agrarian societies simply dissolved. The concept of partible paternity, found across Amazonian cultures and elsewhere, held that multiple men could contribute to the making of a child. A child could have several social fathers, each invested in its survival. Rather than controlling an uncertainty, these societies made it irrelevant. It was an elegant solution, and it worked — for as long as the conditions that made it possible held.

Those conditions did not hold. Agriculture produced surplus, and surplus introduced two distinct pressures that together made the tribal model structurally unworkable — not gradually, but with a kind of inevitability once both were in motion.

The first was cognitive. Human beings can sustain genuine trust-based relationships with roughly 150 people — a limit that appears consistently across anthropological and evolutionary research. Beyond that threshold, the web of mutual accountability that holds communal life together simply cannot be maintained. In a small foraging band, everyone knows everyone: their contributions, their reputation, their history of reciprocity. In an agrarian settlement of several hundred or several thousand, that knowledge is impossible. The social technology that made collective sharing stable breaks down at scale.

The second pressure was economic, and it operated independently of scale. It arrived the moment surplus existed, even in a small group. Once one person could produce significantly more than another — through skill, sustained effort, or the quality of their land — communal pooling stopped being fair in any intuitive sense. The high producer subsidized the low producer invisibly and without recourse. Why invest more than necessary if the rewards are shared equally regardless? The tribal answer to ownership — it belongs to the community — suddenly cost something real and concrete: it made individual investment in surplus production irrational.

These two pressures did not merely converge. They accelerated each other. In an anonymous community where you cannot track who contributed what, the incentive to free-ride increases — and resentment among high producers grows without any mechanism to address it. That economic tension in turn erodes the goodwill that communal life depends on, making the cognitive problem worse. The tribal model did not slowly decline. It became structurally unworkable.

The family unit solved both problems simultaneously. It was small enough to sustain a real trust network — a handful of people whose contributions, characters, and histories were fully known to each other. And it was an incentive structure that made surplus-generating labor personally meaningful across generations: you worked harder because your children would inherit what you built. This is why the family unit felt like a solution rather than an imposition. It was solving genuine, pressing problems that the tribal model could no longer handle.

Women were not simply driven into the pair bond by men. The dissolution of the tribe dissolved something they had depended on too. Female kinship networks — mothers, sisters, aunts living in proximity, sharing the labor of child-rearing, providing protection and counsel — had given women a structural power that operated largely independently of any individual man. Matrilineal descent placed women at the center of social continuity. Collective child-rearing distributed the burden and the risk. In an anonymous agrarian community, all of that thinned out. A dedicated male partner became a genuine survival asset. Women moved toward the pair bond, but they moved into a vacuum — the vacuum left by the collapse of the structures that had sustained them.

But the pair bond was not a symmetrical arrangement. It isolated women from the very structures that had empowered them and placed them in a relation of one-to-one dependency. A woman in a pair bond in an agrarian community had concentrated all her risk into a single man's character, capacity, and survival. This asymmetry was not yet patriarchy — it was not yet encoded in law or explicit ownership — but it created the conditions for patriarchy by stripping women of the collective protection they had previously held.

It is here that Hegel becomes not an external framework but the explanatory engine of what followed. The male hunger for recognition — the need to have one's existence confirmed by another consciousness, which biology had left partially and permanently unsatisfied — found in the pair bond its first institutional vehicle. A woman in one-to-one dependency could be made to confirm a man's significance in a way that the tribe, with its distributed power and female autonomy, never permitted. Control and ownership were not the point. Recognition was the point. Control and ownership were the means.

But the strategy defeated itself from the beginning. A dominated person cannot give genuine recognition — only compliance. The master becomes dependent on the slave for the very thing domination prevents the slave from providing. Men who built patriarchal structures to feel that they counted ended up surrounded by people structurally incapable of making them feel that they counted. The hunger was never fed. It was only institutionalized.

The pair bond and patriarchy did not arrive in sequence. One created the vulnerability. The other moved into it.

They remain conceptually separable, and that distinction matters. The pair bond was a response to real structural problems — the collapse of tribal scale, the breakdown of communal incentives, the thinning of female kinship networks. Patriarchy was the specific system of control, ownership, and legal subordination that crystallized within and around it, driven by the male hunger for recognition finding its institutional form. One need not have produced the other. That they did is a historical fact, not a logical necessity — which is why the distinction holds open a different possibility.

Engels named the mechanism precisely: the patriarchal family as property logistics. Once there were herds, land, and stored surplus to transmit across generations, paternity ceased to be a philosophical question and became a practical one. Control female sexuality, define the legitimate heir, establish the family unit as the atom of ownership. The patriarchal family is, in this reading, a bookkeeping system — not an expression of timeless male nature but a historically specific crystallization of existing anxieties under new material conditions. The psychological fuel — the existential gap, the hunger for recognition — was always there. Property gave it a structure to burn through.

Hegel's further insight points toward what might have been built instead. It is the slave — through work, through transforming the world with their hands — who develops a self-relation the master never achieves. The one excluded from direct creation who builds their way into significance through labor, skill, and care finds something more durable than what domination could ever deliver. This was always available as an answer to the original asymmetry. It remains available. The pre-agrarian man who hunted skillfully, who was trusted by his band, who participated in collective initiation and collective child-rearing — he was already living it, before property made a different answer seem possible.

The modern echo of all this takes a particular form. Industrial and post-industrial society dismantled the two primary pre-agrarian signals of male worth — provisioning and community embeddedness — without replacing them. Work became anonymous and abstract. A salary is invisible where a hunter's catch was immediately legible to the whole band. A man who is good at his job in a large organization demonstrates almost nothing that the people around him can directly read and confirm. Urbanization and mobility dissolved the stable communities in which reputation could accumulate. Status symbols grew loud to compensate: wealth performed rather than demonstrated, titles and possessions standing in for the direct legibility that agrarian and post-agrarian life eliminated.

And underneath it all, the patriarchal project stands revealed as self-defeating — not only morally but structurally. Domination could never deliver genuine recognition. Controlled women, subordinated legally and economically, could only ever return compliance. The institutional form that male anxiety took in agrarian civilization failed on its own terms. It did not resolve the existential gap. It organized an entire social world around the attempt, and the attempt did not work.

None of this is fate for any individual. Here the historical and the philosophical converge on something more personal. Awareness of a deterministic force — evolutionary, psychological, historical — is itself the first act of freedom. One cannot choose what shaped them. But one can choose their relationship to it: to act with it, against it, or with indifference. The pattern does not disappear upon being seen. But it loses its invisibility, which is the condition of its power. To see it clearly is already to stand at a slight distance from it — and in that distance, however small, is where everything begins.
