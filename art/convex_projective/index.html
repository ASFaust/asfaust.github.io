Convex Projective Tori

The Kleinian limit sets of Indra’s Pearls come from deforming a group of Möbius maps. Here the same punctured torus is deformed in a larger geometry, the projective plane, and its limit set becomes the boundary of a convex domain: a curve that is smooth to first order and nowhere smoother.

From the disk to convex domains

In the Klein model, the hyperbolic plane is a disk, its straight lines are chords and its isometries are the projective maps of the plane that keep the disk in place: matrices in SO(2,1), a small part of PGL(3,ℝ). A hyperbolic punctured torus is the disk modulo a group generated by two such maps, a and b. Start with the most symmetric one, the commutator subgroup of the modular group, and move a and b within all of PGL(3,ℝ). For a whole family of such moves the group still preserves a convex domain Ω and acts on it with the same quotient, now a convex projective torus. Ω is no longer an ellipse: its boundary has a tangent line at every point, but, by results of Benoist for closed surfaces and of Crampon and Marquis for surfaces with cusps, it is far rougher than a conic, which it can only be when the group lies in SO(2,1). Distances in Ω are measured by its Hilbert metric, for which the group acts by isometries and the chords are the geodesics, as in the disk.

Fock–Goncharov coordinates

Grandma’s recipe in Indra’s Pearls makes two Möbius maps from two traces. The projective analogue is due to Fock and Goncharov. Cut the torus along three edges into two ideal triangles, T1 and T2. In the universal cover these become the Farey tessellation, whose corners are the fractions, and each corner carries a flag: a point of the boundary of Ω together with the tangent line there. Three flags have a projective invariant, the triple ratio, and the two flags at the ends of an edge, with the two across from it, have two cross-ratios, one at each end, of the four lines through that end. Up to the group there is one triple ratio for each triangle and two edge ratios for each edge, eight numbers in all. Fock and Goncharov showed that any eight positive numbers arise, each from exactly one group with its flags, and that this group is discrete and faithful and preserves a convex domain. So unlike the traces of a Kleinian group, every choice works, and the sliders range over all of them.

With all eight equal to 1 the flags lie on a conic and Ω is the disk. With the triple ratios 1 and the two ratios of each edge equal, the group stays in SO(2,1): these are the other hyperbolic tori, the Farey triangles sheared along their edges, and Ω stays an ellipse. Everything else is a genuinely projective torus. The loop ABab around the puncture has eigenvalue ratios z₁z₂⋯z₆ and z₁z₂⋯z₆ / (t₁t₂)³, where the z are the edge ratios and the t the triple ratios, so it is unipotent, a cusp as in the hyperbolic torus, exactly when both products are 1. Otherwise the puncture opens into a hole. The fans of triangles at each corner then pile up along a straight piece of the boundary between the two fixed points of a conjugate of the loop, and the limit set of the group, a Cantor set, is what remains of the curve between these gaps.

Drawing the curve

The flag at a fraction follows from its Farey parents: the flags at the two ends of an edge and at the far corner of the triangle beyond it fix the flag at the mediant, given that edge’s two ratios and the next triangle’s triple ratio. Going down the Farey tree in order walks once around the boundary of Ω. Below an edge, the arc of the boundary lies in the triangle formed by the chord and the two tangent lines at its ends, so once each end is closer than ε/4 to the tangent at the other, the chord stands for the arc and the search backs up. The same triangle tells which branches leave the view, so a zoomed picture costs little more than the whole. Each node of the search is a triangle of the tessellation, and with straight sides these tile Ω.

Near a cusp the flags of a branch nearly coincide, and building each from the last would soon lose all precision. So the search carries group elements instead. Every Farey edge, seen from one side, is the image of one of six edges near the start, and the matrix that moves it there is updated one letter at a time, much like a word in the Kleinian search. Only the flag at the mediant is moved by that matrix, since the ends of a deep edge may sit near its repelling fixed point, where it would crush them; the ends are handed down from the parents. Around a hole, the triangles of a fan pile up along a straight gap and the chord across it never gets shorter, so a chord that keeps returning unchanged is taken as straight.

The picture

Projective maps keep lines and conics but not angles or lengths, so a picture of Ω depends on the chart. This one divides by a line that misses Ω, the sum of four tangent lines, and then moves Ω by an affine map so that its centroid is at the centre and its second moments are equal, as round as it can be. The Hilbert balls shade the natural metric of Ω, in which the projective maps of the group are isometries and the straight lines are geodesics: the distance from the centre to a point x, where the line through them leaves Ω at a behind the centre and b beyond x, is ½ log(|ax| |bc| / (|ac| |bx|)). For the disk this is the hyperbolic metric, with round balls. As Ω tends to a triangle they become hexagons.
