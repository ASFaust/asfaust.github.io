Buddhabrot Explorer

The Mandelbrot set, drawn by where its escaping points travel. Every pixel counts how many orbits passed through it.

Orbits

The Mandelbrot set asks, for each point c of the complex plane, whether the orbit

z₀ = 0
zₙ₊₁ = zₙ² + c

stays bounded. The usual picture colours each c by how quickly its orbit escapes. The Buddhabrot looks at the orbits themselves: every c that escapes leaves a trail of points z₁, z₂, …, and each of those adds one to a counter at its pixel. Millions of trails pile up into the seated figure that gives the image its name. A trail is followed until it leaves a circle that holds the whole picture, so even the corners get the last points of escaping orbits. The real axis runs from top to bottom here, so the figure sits upright.

Sampling the boundary

Most random points escape after a few steps and only add noise near the centre. The interesting orbits are long, and they start right at the edge of the set. So the page does not sample the plane at random. It first lays a fine grid over the set and tests every grid point; a cell whose corners disagree, some inside the set and some outside, lies on its boundary. The “near the boundary” sampler spreads its points evenly over a thin band of such cells, softened at the edges so that no squares show. The bisection sampler goes further. Each of its samples starts as two corners of one boundary cell, one inside the set and one outside. A random point near the middle of the pair then replaces whichever end is on the same side, over and over. After a few dozen of these steps the outside point sits a hair's breadth from the set, where orbits take the longest to escape.

Optional nearby points scatter a Gaussian cloud around each boundary sample and add the orbits of those that escape too: similar orbits, slightly shifted, which soften the points a long orbit revisits again and again. Everything after the histogram, from scaling and clipping to blur and colour, runs instantly in your browser without recomputing the orbits. The heavy part is spread over Web Workers, one per CPU core.

Colour by orbit length

The classic way to colour a Buddhabrot, sometimes called the “Nebulabrot”, sorts the orbits by how long they take to escape. Here there are three channels: the orbits that escape within a few dozen steps, those that escape within a few hundred, and all of them. Each channel is a Buddhabrot of its own, gets its own colour, and the three colours add up. Short orbits stay near the outside and draw a soft halo, long orbits trace the fine structure close to the set, and where all of them pile up the picture turns white.

Zoomed views

Zooming in breaks this. The orbits that cross a small window mostly start far away from it, and only a tiny fraction of all orbits cross it at all. The Metropolis method samples where it matters: it runs many random walks over the starting points c, each one trying a small jump (or, rarely, a jump anywhere) and keeping it with a probability that depends on how many of the new orbit's points land in the view. Walks therefore linger on the orbits that draw the window. Each orbit is weighted by one over its number of hits in the view, which undoes that preference, so the result is the same picture the whole-set render shows in that window, only with far less noise. A view that straddles the real axis also counts the mirror image of every orbit, since the picture is symmetric there. An orbit that grazes the view with one or two points puts all its weight on them, so a walk that lingers on one leaves a bright speck; “Remove specks in zooms” flattens those, and “Smooth faint areas in zooms” blurs each place until it holds enough orbit points, so the faint parts lose their grain while the bright detail stays sharp. This needs to know how densely the starting points are spread, which is why Metropolis works with the “near the boundary” sampler but not with the bisection one. The walks start from starting points whose orbits reach the view, picked by their number of hits, and the zoom borrows the brightness of the last whole-set render, so it keeps its look.
