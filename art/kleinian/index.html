Kleinian Limit Sets

Two Möbius maps of the plane, applied in every possible order, pile up on a fractal: the limit set of the group they generate. Choose the traces of the two maps and watch it form.

How it works

A Möbius map sends a point z of the plane to (pz + q) / (rz + s). It moves circles to circles, and it is determined up to scale by the matrix [p q; r s]. Take two such maps a and b, together with their inverses A and B, and compose them in every order: a, ab, abA, abAb, … The points that these words pile up on, however long they get, form the limit set of the group.

Most choices of maps give a chaotic mess. The beautiful ones are those where the commutator abAB is parabolic, a map with a single fixed point. Then the plane folds into two punctured tori glued at their cusps, the group is quasi-Fuchsian, and the limit set is a single closed fractal curve. Up to a change of coordinates such a pair of maps is fixed by two complex numbers, the traces p + s of a and b. “Grandma’s recipe” from Indra’s Pearls turns the two traces into matrices.

Drawing the curve

Every point of the limit set has an address, an infinite word in a, b, A, B in which no letter is followed by its inverse. The search walks the tree of these words depth first, always turning the same way, so it travels once around the curve in order. Below a word W it compares two points: W applied to the fixed points of the two commutators that can follow its last letter. These are the two ends of the piece of curve under W. Once they are less than ε apart it draws a line between them and backs up. Otherwise it goes one letter deeper.

Where the curve passes through a parabolic point the words converge slowly, so the depth is capped. With “follow narrow necks” the search also checks the fixed point of the last letter itself, the tip of the whorl, so it does not cut across a frond attached by a thin neck. For a zoomed view, a first coarse pass measures which part of the plane each pair of opening letters fills. Any branch that maps its part completely outside the view is skipped, so a deep zoom costs about as much as the full picture. The work is split over four Web Workers, one for each first letter.

The random walk

The second method, after the stochastic program in Chris King’s toolbox, needs no tree at all. It starts from a few points known to lie on the limit set, the fixed points of the generators and of the commutators, and moves them all together by one generator after another, chosen at random. The next letter is never the inverse of the last, which would only undo it, and with the chance set by “repeat a letter” it is the same letter again, so the walk spirals far into the whorls around the generators’ fixed points. Every image of a point of the limit set lies on the limit set again, so every step plots a few more of its points. Each point is coloured by the word that carried it there, read from the letter applied last, just like a segment of the search.

The walk never knows when it is done, and it fills the dense parts of the set long before it reaches the thin ones, so the curve comes out speckled rather than drawn, and more steps fill in the thin parts only slowly. In return it works for any group: it does not care whether the words contract, so it also gives a picture where the search would never end, for groups near the edge of the discrete region, and even beyond it, where the orbits no longer settle on a fractal and smear into a cloud. It also gains nothing from zooming, since most of its points then fall outside the view.

The random descent

The third method sits between the two. Like the search, it builds each word one letter at a time from the left and stops once the piece of the curve below it is shorter than ε, drawing the same segment the search would. But instead of trying every branch it follows a single random one, then starts again from the top. Of the three letters that may come next, it picks each with a chance proportional to the size of the piece of limit set below it, measured by where the word sends the fixed points that can follow. So big pieces get many samples and small ones few, and the picture fills in all over at once. Because no descent goes deeper than the maximum word length, it always ends, even for a group that is not discrete.

The sampling is not quite even. The pieces below a word together measure more than the word’s own piece, and that surplus compounds with every letter, so branches that need long words, the narrow necks into cusps and the turns of a spiral, are still visited less often than they should be.

The Maskit slice

Fix the trace of b at 2, so b is parabolic, and vary the trace of a. The values that give a discrete group form the Maskit slice, the shaded region of the first trace plane. Its edge is fractal, and on it sit the cusp groups: for each fraction p/q a particular word W(p/q) becomes parabolic too, and the limit set pinches into chains of tangent circles with period q. The trace of W(p/q) is a polynomial of degree q in the trace of a. The explorer builds it along the Farey tree with the identity tr uv + tr uV = tr u · tr v, then finds the right root with Newton’s method. Fractions converging to an irrational number lead to degenerate groups, whose limit sets fill whole regions.

For other values of tr b no such formula is known, so the trace planes test each point numerically. They run the same search, coarsely and without drawing, and measure distances on the Riemann sphere rather than in the plane, so the size and position of the limit set do not matter. Each point is searched twice, the second time with half the tolerance. For a discrete group that multiplies the work by about 2D, where D ≤ 2 is the dimension of its limit set. A group that is not discrete may look tidy at the coarse scale, but its words stop contracting below it, and the second search runs out of its budget. The number of steps rises towards the edge of the discrete region, which gives the shading. It is a numerical test, not a proof, but with tr b = 2 its edge lies on the exact curve of cusps.

With the commutator trace set to 0 instead of −2, (abAB)² is the identity, the group is no longer free, and many words name the same map. A small finite-state automaton from Indra’s Pearls then tells the search which words to skip.

Other relations work the same way, given their automata. Chris King made them with Alun Williams’ program MAF for (abAB)³ = I, commutator trace −1, and for (abAB)⁵ = b⁴ = I, where the commutator has a square root of order five, and the recipe takes any commutator trace. When the commutator is elliptic, it turns the plane about its fixed point, but Grandma’s normalization hides that rotation. The symmetric normalization takes AB and A as the generators instead, and moves them by the Möbius map [1 1; −1 1], so the rotation shows as a symmetry of the picture. With trace −1 it is six-fold. Some of these groups converge only for short words, and then the branches that reach the maximum length are best left out.

The quincuncial view

A Möbius map acts on the whole Riemann sphere, and a limit set often runs off to infinity, so the plane only ever shows part of it. Charles Peirce’s quincuncial projection maps the entire sphere conformally onto a square. The disk around the limit set becomes the northern hemisphere and goes onto the inscribed diamond, by the elliptic integral ∫ dt / √(1 − t⁴). The rest of the sphere, ∞ included, fills the four corners, and ∞ itself sits at the corners of the square. The search runs as usual, but each point is projected before ε is measured, so the detail stays even across the square. The squares tile the plane, each neighbour turned by 180°, so a curve that leaves through one edge returns through the mirror point of the same edge. Being conformal, the projection keeps small circles round, but it cannot be a Möbius map itself: it bends the large ones.

Line drawings

The search draws nothing but straight segments, so the picture can also be saved as a line drawing, for a pen plotter, a cutter or a 3D program. The SVG export runs the search again with the settings on screen and measures the result in millimetres. Each colour becomes a layer, which a plotter can draw with its own pen, and there is no background. Many segments share their ends, and where the curve touches itself several meet at one point. The export joins them into as few paths as possible: it pairs up the points where an odd number of segments meet and walks an Euler circuit through the rest (Hierholzer’s algorithm), so the pen draws each piece once and seldom lifts. The random walk plots points instead of lines, so it has no line drawing.
