Markov Julia Sets

The Kleinian limit sets come from two Möbius maps. Take instead the space of all such pairs and the moves that rearrange them: iterating one move gives a dynamical system on a complex surface, with Julia sets of its own, and all the moves together draw a set much like the Mandelbrot set.

The Markov surfaces

A pair of matrices A, B in SL(2,ℂ) is determined, up to a change of coordinates, by three traces: x = tr A, y = tr B and z = tr AB. They always satisfy x² + y² + z² − xyz = k, where k = tr ABA⁻¹B⁻¹ + 2. For each k this cubic equation defines a complex surface S_k. With k = 0 it is, up to scale, Markov’s equation from number theory, and it holds the groups of Indra’s Pearls, whose commutator is parabolic with trace −2.

Given y and z, the equation is quadratic in x, so each point has a partner with the other root, yz − x. Swapping them is the move s₁, and s₂ and s₃ do the same for y and z. In terms of the matrices they are changes of generators: s₁ replaces the pair (A, B) by (AB², B⁻¹), which has the traces (yz − x, y, z). Together they generate, up to finite index, the action of the extended mapping class group of the punctured torus on its characters. The tree of Markov triples is the orbit of (3, 3, 3) under them.

The Julia set of one word

Pick a word in the three moves, such as s₁s₂s₁s₃, and apply it over and over. Most points run off to infinity, doubly exponentially fast, since each move multiplies two coordinates. Some stay bounded forever. As for a polynomial in one variable, the edge between the two kinds, the Julia set, is where the dynamics is chaotic, and it is a fractal. Cantat studied these maps in depth: when the word uses all three moves the map is hyperbolic, with chaotic dynamics on a fractal Julia set. A word with only two moves keeps one coordinate fixed and just turns the conic it lies on, so it has no Julia set worth the name.

Is there a Mandelbrot set?

Not in the strict sense. The Mandelbrot set collects the parameters c whose Julia set is connected, and it is found by following the one critical point of z² + c. These maps are invertible, like the Hénon maps, and have no critical points to follow. But there is a close companion, drawn with “Draw: where every word escapes”. Instead of one word, take all of them at once. The orbit of a point under the whole group lies on a tree: each triple has three neighbours, one for each move, and every coordinate met along the way is the trace of a simple closed curve on the torus. Bowditch singled out the points where this tree behaves as it does for a Kleinian group: no trace lies in [−2, 2], and only finitely many are small, so that every path out along the tree escapes. There every word escapes, so this Bowditch set never meets a Julia set. Outside it the group acts chaotically, and the Julia sets of all the words lie there, drawn in black. For k = 0 the Bowditch set contains the quasi-Fuchsian groups, whose limit sets are fractal circles like those of the Kleinian page, and Bowditch conjectured that it is exactly those. Its edge is a fractal, with the scalloped look of the slices of Kleinian groups in Indra’s Pearls.

To test a point the page walks down the tree, always by the move that shrinks its coordinate the most, until no move does, and then visits every vertex it can reach through small coordinates, below max(8, 2 + 3√|k|). For a Bowditch point that is a short, finite walk. More than 100 steps down, a path through more than 40 small coordinates, more than 300 vertices in all, or a trace within 10−7 of [−2, 2], and the point counts as chaotic. The set is drawn on one surface only: along a line through ℂ³, k grows without bound, and so does the walk.

Slices

The surface has two complex dimensions, four real ones, so a picture shows a slice. The first kind is a conic of one surface: fix z = z₀ and write z₀ = λ + 1/λ. The equation becomes (x − λy)(x − y/λ) = k − z₀², so u = x − λy runs over the punctured plane and names every point of the conic, and the picture is the u plane. The second kind is a line in ℂ³: fix y and z and let x range over the plane. Its points lie on different surfaces, as k changes with x.

Drawing

The colouring is that of the Minimal Mandelbrot. For one word, each sample is iterated letter by letter until the largest coordinate passes 10⁸, or until the maximum number of words. The escape time is smoothed by where the escape radius falls between the last two letters on a doubly logarithmic scale. The distance estimate follows the derivatives of x, y and z along the slice, and is |v| log |v| / |dv| for the coordinate v that escaped. For all words, the value is log(1 + 1/ℓ), where ℓ = 2 log |λ|, λ + 1/λ = t, is the shortest translation length among the traces t met on the walk, and the distance estimate is ℓ over its gradient.

The grey value starts as that value, rescaled so that the fastest escape is white and the slowest black, and within one sample of the border it is lowered to the distance estimate itself, never darker than 0.2 far from it. It then runs along a ramp in OKLCh, at one hue, from the colour at the set to the colour far out. The bounded or chaotic points are black, or white with “Invert”. Each pixel averages its samples in OKLab. The work is shared between Web Workers, which keep their samples, so a new colour needs no new iterations.

“New place” picks a random map. For one word it walks towards the Julia set, always to a nearby point that escapes later, with steps that shrink with the distance estimate. For all words it looks on a coarse grid for chaotic points with chaotic neighbours, which lie in a region of positive area, and halves the way from one of them to a Bowditch neighbour. It then zooms in, evenly on a logarithmic scale. The seed fixes all of these choices.
