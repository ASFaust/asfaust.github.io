Conway Polyhedra

A handful of simple operations, applied to a handful of simple solids, give almost every polyhedron with a name, and endlessly many without one.

Operators

John Conway wrote polyhedra as a seed, a capital letter, with operators in front of it: T C O D I are the five Platonic solids, P5 A5 Y5 U5 the pentagonal prism, antiprism, pyramid and cupola, and J27 the 27th Johnson solid. The operators act from right to left, so tkD is the dodecahedron (D) with a pyramid raised on each face (k, kis), with every vertex then cut off (t, truncate).

The basic operators are dual d, which swaps faces and vertices; ambo a, which cuts every vertex down to the midpoints of its edges; truncate t; kis k; join j, a four-sided face across every edge; gyro g and propeller p, which give everything a twist; and chamfer c, which turns every edge into a hexagon. The others are made from them: expand e = aa, ortho o = jj, bevel b = ta, meta m = kj, needle n = kd, zip z = dk, snub s = dg. r is the mirror image, which only changes the chiral shapes. A number limits t and k to vertices or faces of that degree: k4 raises pyramids only on the squares.

All 13 Archimedean solids are one or two operators away from a Platonic solid (tI is the football, sC the snub cube), and their duals, the Catalan solids, are a d further. Goldberg polyhedra and geodesic spheres are short strings too: tI, cD, tkD.

Only the graph

The operators work on the combinatorics alone: which vertices lie on which face, in which order around it. The page stores every face as a list of vertices running counter-clockwise when seen from outside, and splits each edge into two half-edges, one per side. Each operator then builds its new faces from these half-edges without looking at any coordinates, and so the faces come out with the right orientation, every time.

Colour by origin shows the bookkeeping. Faces that grow out of an old face keep its colour, faces that grow around an old vertex take the colour of that vertex, and faces that grow out of edges get a new colour. In tC the octagons are the old faces of the cube, the triangles its old corners.

Back into space

The graph alone says nothing about where the vertices should go. Steinitz’s theorem says that the graphs of convex polyhedra are exactly the planar 3-connected graphs, and every operator here keeps a graph planar and 3-connected, so a convex shape always exists. A stronger theorem, by Koebe, Andreev and Thurston, picks out a particularly nice one: the canonical polyhedron, whose edges all touch one sphere, whose faces are flat, and whose points of contact are centred on the middle of the sphere. It is unique up to rotation and has every symmetry the graph has.

The page finds it with George Hart’s iteration: move both ends of each edge so that the edge’s point closest to the centre moves towards the unit sphere, recentre, move each vertex towards the planes of its faces, and repeat. Two changes make it reliable. Hart takes the closest point on the whole line through an edge; the page takes it on the edge itself, since otherwise the apex of a flat pyramid runs away to infinity. And each round also continues a little in the direction of the last one, which makes the slow, large-scale adjustments of big shapes many times faster. The shape is relaxed after each operator, so that the next one starts from a good shape, and the canonical optimizer then finishes the job while you watch.

Each optimizer is a button, and each starts from wherever the vertices are, so they can be chained: canonical, then regular faces, then flat faces gives a different shape from regular faces alone. Clicking another button while one runs switches over on the spot; clicking the running one stops it there. The list of steps under the buttons goes back: click a step, and the shape is as it was after it, at once, with the later steps dropped. The URL records the chain, with the round at which a step was stopped, and since every optimizer is deterministic, a link replays to exactly the same shape. Trying buttons one after another leaves many steps that later ones undo, so “Share link” first looks for a shorter chain: it replays the last few steps on their own, then the chain with each step left out, and keeps a shorter chain when it ends at the same shape, up to a rotation. Optimizers that run to the end forget most of where they started, so often only the last two or three steps are needed. A new polyhedron starts as built, with an empty chain.

Three buttons give a fresh start instead of an optimizer: “As built” puts the vertices back where the operators put them, “Random” scatters them as a cloud of random points, from which the optimizers find their own way (a random start has no symmetry, so none is kept), and “Spectral” places them from the eigenvectors of the graph, described below. “Shake” moves every vertex a little at random from where it is, to see whether a shape is stable or to knock an optimizer off a balance point. Two switches act on the next optimizer you start. Whiten acts after every round: it makes the x, y and z coordinates uncorrelated and equally spread, by the inverse square root of their covariance matrix, so the shape cannot collapse into a plane or a line. It is an affine map, and affine faces commutes with it: what it settles to stays settled. The other optimizers are pushed somewhere else, which is the point. Whitening commutes with rotations, so it never breaks a symmetry. Keep symmetry averages the shape over its symmetries every few rounds; without it, small asymmetries from rounding can grow into a lopsided shape.

The canonical shape can be extreme. In tttttD the twelve big faces take up nearly everything, and the small ones shrink to slivers. Regular faces tries something else: make every face a regular polygon. Each round gives every face its own copies of its vertices, moves each face on its own towards the regular polygon that fits it best, and then puts every vertex at the mean of its copies. It often succeeds, and the price is convexity: where the corners of regular polygons around a vertex add up to more than a full turn, the vertex has to become a saddle, and the shape folds. The number in its button blends between only flattening each face (0) and making it regular (1). Affine faces is the same scheme with a looser target: each face becomes the affine image of a regular polygon, evenly spaced points on an ellipse, which is where repeatedly taking the midpoints of a polygon’s edges ends up. Triangles are always affine-regular, so they only follow their neighbours. Flat faces moves each vertex towards the planes of its faces and nothing else, so it keeps the shape close to where it was. Face mean puts every vertex at the mean of the centres of its faces, again and again. That is the power method for the matrix doing it, so on its own the shape flattens into a plane or a line, its strongest pattern; with Orthogonalise and Normalise on it keeps three patterns apart, and settles on the spectral layout of the graph, which PolyOp used to make its seeds. Its faces need not be flat. Equal edges is the same scheme with edges instead of faces: each edge, on its own, is stretched or shrunk about its midpoint to the mean length.

The Mix runs several of these at once. Each target puts its own copies of the vertices where it wants them, and every vertex goes to the weighted mean of all its copies, so the weights say how much each one counts, and the shape settles where their pulls balance. Regular 0.4 with flat 0.6 is the same as regular faces at 0.4; flat with equal edges, or face mean with equal edges, are shapes no single button makes. Besides the targets above there are tangent edges, the canonical iteration's step that moves each edge until it touches a common sphere, and sphere, which pushes every vertex to the same distance from the centre. Changing a weight while a mix runs carries on from the shape as it is.

Spectral starts come straight from the graph. Write down its adjacency matrix, with a 1 wherever two vertices share an edge, and take three of its eigenvectors as the x, y and z coordinates. The dropdown offers three relatives too: the Laplacian, the degrees minus the adjacency, whose smoothest eigenvectors have the smallest eigenvalues; vertex mean, which moves each vertex to the mean of its neighbours; and face mean, to the mean of its faces’ centres. Where every vertex has the same number of edges, the first three agree. Every symmetry of the graph only permutes the vertices, and that permutation commutes with the matrix, so each eigenspace is carried into itself, and a layout from whole eigenspaces is exactly symmetric. The page keeps the ones in which every symmetry acts as a different rotation or reflection, and lists them from the smoothest down. The first is often convex, on about half of a random sample, the Laplacian slightly more often than the others. Later ones are often stellations: for the dodecahedron, the eigenvalue √5 gives the dodecahedron itself and −√5 the great stellated dodecahedron, the same graph with pentagrams for faces, winding seven times round its centre. The number by the button steps through them, and the note says how often each winds round. Star faces then makes every face a regular star polygon: a face with n sides becomes {n/k}, its corners joined every k-th, with k nearest the pointedness times n, so that at 0.3 pentagons become pentagrams and decagons {10/3}. Triangles, squares and hexagons have no star and stay regular. A convex face has nothing of a star in it, so from a convex shape it cannot even start; from a stellated spectral start it finds the uniform star polyhedra, such as the great stellated truncated dodecahedron from tD, with every edge the same length. Stellate finds such a start without the list. Affine faces keeps the part of each face that goes once round evenly, an ellipse polygon; stellate keeps the part that goes round k times, the star {n/k} stretched by an affine map. That is a linear map of the positions, so with whitening every round it settles, like face mean, on three eigenvectors: those of the layouts whose faces are closest to affine stars. On D that is the great stellated dodecahedron itself. A convex face has nothing of a star in it, so stellate first shakes the shape a little. Where the faces have no star polygon, as triangles, squares and hexagons do not, it finds no stellation.

The readable shape asks for something weaker than regular faces, and so can keep the shape convex and flat. A face reads wrong when two of its corners nearly merge, so that a hexagon looks like a pentagon, or when a corner is nearly straight. So it asks that the edges of each face be about equally long and that every corner turn at least 60% as much as a regular polygon’s corner does; how big each face is may vary as much as the graph needs. Where that still leaves a choice, as in czD, whose hexagons can all have equal edges in many ways, it prefers corners that are alike, so that faces come out as regular as the shape allows. It changes the shape in a way that cannot bend a face: when every vertex has three edges, as after a truncation, it moves the planes of the faces and puts each vertex where its three planes meet; when every face is a triangle, it moves the vertices, since a triangle is always flat. Any step that would dent the shape is refused, so it needs a convex shape to start from: the current one if it is convex, else the one the operators built, else it first runs the canonical optimizer until the shape is convex. On other shapes it runs the canonical optimizer instead. On tttttD the faces next to the big ones become even ovals instead of spiky slivers. Not every graph can be made fully readable: where many triangles meet at one corner of a convex shape, some of them must be thin.

Symmetry

A symmetry of the graph, an automorphism, is pinned down by where it sends a single half-edge and whether it keeps or reverses the orientation. So the page tries every half-edge that looks alike, grows the map from there along the faces, and keeps the ones that close up. The canonical polyhedron shows every automorphism as a rotation or reflection, so fitting a matrix to each names the point group, shown under the picture as its Schoenflies symbol: Ih for the full symmetry of the icosahedron, I for its rotations alone, D5h for a pentagonal prism. Colouring by symmetry orbit gives faces that the symmetries carry into each other the same colour. The orientations in the Scene panel come from the same symmetries: the faces, vertices and edges fall into orbits, and every orbit that sits on a rotation axis is a way to stand the shape, labelled by how many times a turn about that axis brings it back to itself. Every optimizer averages the shape over all its symmetries every few rounds; that stops it drifting off and makes regular faces settle much sooner.

Drawing

The edges are painted onto the faces rather than built as tubes. Every triangle on the screen knows its distance to the nearby sides of its face, and the pixels closer than the line width take the edge colour. The width is measured on the shape, not on the screen, so an export at 8192 pixels looks like the view, only sharper. Drawn on the screen, the edges are instead lines of the same width everywhere, laid over the picture like ink. Which parts of a line are hidden is decided by drawing the faces once more, each in a colour that is its number: a point of an edge counts as seen when a pixel beside it belongs to one of the edge's own two faces. So the lines keep their full width at the outline and in the valleys, and with the faces turned off the hidden ones can be shown faintly, or left out.

Origin

This is a port of PolyOp, an earlier C++ library with Python bindings, rewritten from scratch around half-edges. The regular-faces optimizer (there called detached faces) and the spectral layouts come from there.
